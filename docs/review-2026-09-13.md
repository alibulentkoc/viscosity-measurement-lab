Repository: https://github.com/alibulentkoc/viscosity-measurement-lab

HTML handouts, the simulator physics block, and the four test suites. All

smoke-answers.js (31), smoke-grader.js (29). Every check passed.

## 1. Verdict in one paragraph

The physics is correct and the package is unusually well built for a
teaching lab: three instruments, one shared physics block, an answer key
that computes from master values, a grader that recomputes every answer,
and a test suite that extracts and executes the physics in isolation. The
central lesson (Stokes' law does not apply to the bench sphere, report the
number anyway, state its limitation) is exactly the right lesson and is
carried through consistently. There is one accuracy problem worth fixing
(the two bench fluids are listed in the fluid library at their invalid
Stokes viscosities, which are 4 to 8 times too high), one presentation
problem (rendered em dashes in the handouts), and a handful of smaller
items. None of them blocks classroom use.

## 3. The one accuracy problem to fix

### 3.1 The bench fluids carry their invalid Stokes viscosities as reference values

In the simulator's FLUIDS array:

    15W-40 Motor Oil   mu: 1.2826 Pa.s   src: "bench"
    Dexron II ATF      mu: 0.5448 Pa.s   src: "bench"

Those are the falling-ball results, which the README, the handout, and the
answer key all say are wrong because Re is far above the creeping-flow
limit. Typical published values at 20 C are about 0.26 to 0.30 Pa.s for a
15W-40 (about 300 cSt, from the 40 C and 100 C grade points through ASTM
D341) and about 0.06 to 0.09 Pa.s for a Dexron II type ATF. The library
values are therefore roughly 4 times (15W-40) and 6 to 8 times (ATF) too
high.

Consequences a student will see:

- On the comparison tab, 15W-40 plots above castor oil (0.985 Pa.s) and
  near glycerol (1.41 Pa.s). Dexron II plots above SAE 90 gear oil (0.28
  Pa.s). Both are wrong by inspection for anyone who has handled the
  fluids.
- The Saybolt station's fallback prediction for 15W-40 (when no rotary or
  Saybolt reading has been entered) is about 6700 s, which the tool then
  flags as beyond the working range. The flag is doing its job, but the
  cause is the bad reference value, not the method.
- The falling-ball animation clock 2 ("class reference time") is fine,
  because that is a genuine timing. The problem is only in mu.

Suggested fix: keep t_measured (it is a real measurement) but replace mu
with a published value at a stated refT, and add tempPts from the grade
sheet so the Walther fit works for the bench fluids. For a generic
15W-40: [[40, 105], [100, 14.5]] cSt; for a Dexron II type ATF: about
[[40, 37], [100, 7.5]] cSt. Mark src as "published" or "grade". Then the
comparison chart, the Saybolt prediction, and the Reynolds check all line
up with the lesson instead of contradicting it.

### 3.2 The Reynolds number in Section 5.6 is self-referential

Re is computed with the Stokes mu. Because that mu is too high, Re comes
out too low. For 15W-40 the sheet reports Re = 4 (MARGINAL); with the true
viscosity Re is about 17 (INVALID). For ATF the sheet reports Re = 22; the
true figure is about 170. The classification of 15W-40 as merely marginal
is an artifact.

This is actually a good teaching point, and it costs one line to add to
Section 5.6 or to Question 10: "Recompute Re using the rotary viscosity at
the same temperature. This is the honest check, because it does not use
the number you are testing." The simulator could do the same automatically
whenever a rotary reading exists for the same fluid.

## 4. Smaller items

### 4.1 Rendered em dashes

The LaTeX section titles use "---" (Part I --- Falling Ball Viscometer)
and the safety line uses "50--60", so the PDF prints em and en dashes. The
HTML handout, answer sheet, grader, simulator and index carry 36 em-dash
entities between them. If ASCII-only output is a house rule, replace with
a colon in titles ("Part I: Falling Ball Viscometer") and "to" in ranges.

### 4.2 Two sentences that contradict each other

Core Principle: "None of the three instruments on this bench measures that
resistance directly." Part III: "A rotary viscometer measures the absolute
viscosity of a fluid directly." The second is the looser statement. The
rotary instrument measures torque at a set speed and applies a spindle
factor, which the handout itself explains three paragraphs later. Suggest
"comes closest to measuring viscosity directly" or "reports viscosity
directly."

### 4.3 Regime labels are generous

Oseen's correction gives a drag about 19 percent above Stokes at Re = 1
and about 2 times at Re = 10. Labelling Re below 1 as "valid" is common
teaching practice, but a student who lands at Re = 0.8 will believe the
number to within a few percent when the true error is near 15 percent.
Consider "good (Re < 0.1), usable with up to about 20 percent error
(0.1 to 1), marginal (1 to 10), invalid (10 and above)". The thresholds in
the code need not change.

### 4.4 The wall correction is Faxen, not Ladenburg

The series 1 - 2.104 b + 2.09 b^3 (- 0.95 b^5) is Faxen's. Ladenburg's
correction is the simpler v_inf = v (1 + 2.4 d/D). Both are creeping-flow
results, so strictly they carry the same Re limitation as Stokes' law. The
code comment already says "Ladenburg / Faxen"; the README and answer sheet
say only Ladenburg. Rename, and note that with the 12.70 mm sphere the
correction is very large: in a 50 mm bore the factor is 0.50, so the
uncorrected viscosity is about double the corrected one. That is probably
the single biggest reason the bench fall time (1.89 s) is longer than the
free-fall prediction for a typical 15W-40 (about 1.1 s at Re near 30).

### 4.5 Settling distance before the START mark

The handout says to release the sphere at the top of the tube. It would
help to say how far above the mark is enough. A drag-model integration for
the bench sphere gives about 0.2 m to reach 99 percent of terminal
velocity in 15W-40 and about 0.4 to 0.5 m in ATF. If the tube does not
allow that, the ATF timing includes acceleration and reads high on top of
everything else. Worth one sentence and one number.

### 4.6 Saybolt temperature

ASTM D88 specifies 100, 130 or 210 F (37.8, 54.4, 98.9 C). Testing at 50 C
is fine for teaching and the README explains why, but the conversion
formulas are for 100 F. The D2161 temperature factor, 1 + 0.000061 (T -
100 F), is about 0.1 percent at 50 C, so no numerical change is needed; a
footnote saying so would pre-empt a sharp student.

### 4.7 Rotary sweep practicality

500 mL of oil cooling from 60 C to 20 C in still air takes well over an
hour. Either accept a shorter sweep (60 to 35 C) or note that the beaker
should sit in a water bath that the students step down. The answer key's
2.5 C matching window for Q3 is sensible.

### 4.8 Significant figures on the sphere

8.3 g is two figures. A 1/2 inch chrome steel ball is 8.36 g. If the
balance reads to 0.01 g, print 8.36 g; the sphere density then lands at
7795 kg/m3, closer to the handbook value, and the grader's "not steel"
check gets a tighter master.

## 5. Usefulness as a course tool

Strengths:

- Offline, single-file, no build: it will open from an LMS upload or a
  memory stick in ten years.
- One physics block, extracted and tested; duplicated on purpose so each
  file stands alone.
- The amber "reference, not measured" colouring and the source labelling
  on every derived value are exactly what a measurement course should
  model.
- The grader recomputes every answer from the student's own inputs, so a
  student with a bad timing but correct arithmetic gets credit for the
  arithmetic.
- The non-Newtonian entry that refuses to compute is a good move.
- Questions 5, 6, 8 and 10 are the strongest on the sheet. They ask for
  direction, factor and justification rather than a number.

Gaps for the non-engineer audience (the plain-language guide addresses
these):

- No worked numerical example anywhere in the student handout.
- No glossary. "Kinematic", "shear", "creeping flow", "Reynolds number"
  and "centistokes" all appear before they are explained in plain words.
- The falling-ball chain is seven equations for what is, on this bench, a
  single multiplication (mu in Pa.s = 0.68 x fall time in seconds).
- The Reynolds check can be reduced to a rule of thumb for this bench
  (Re = 14 / t^2, so the ball must take longer than about 4 s for the law
  to hold and longer than about 1.2 s for the number to be usable at all).

## 6. Recommended actions, in order

1. Replace the two bench mu values in the fluid library with published
   values and grade-sheet tempPts (Section 3.1).
2. Add the "recompute Re with the rotary viscosity" line (Section 3.2).
3. Strip em and en dashes from the tex and HTML (Section 4.1).
4. Rename Ladenburg to Faxen and add the settling-distance sentence
   (Sections 4.4, 4.5).
5. Fix the "measures directly" wording (Section 4.2).
6. Offer the plain-language guide alongside the handout for the
   Agricultural Mechanization and Business students who are not
   engineering majors.
