2Core Principle

Viscosity is a measure of a fluid's resistance to flow. None of the three instruments on this bench measures that resistance directly. Each one measures something else — how fast a sphere sinks, how long a fixed volume takes to drain, how hard a rotor must be driven — and then infers viscosity from a model. The model is where the physics lives, and the model can fail while the instrument still gives you a perfectly repeatable number.

Two quantities carry the name viscosity and they are not interchangeable. Dynamic (or absolute) viscosity μ measures resistance to shear and is reported in Pa·s or centipoise. Kinematic viscosity ν is dynamic viscosity divided by density and is reported in m²/s or centistokes. Density is the only bridge between them. An ISO hydraulic grade such as ISO 46 refers to kinematic viscosity in centistokes at 40 °C; a Stokes' law result is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s. Quoting one where the other is expected is the most common error in this laboratory.

What you should be able to say at the end
Which of your three measurements you trust most, and why. That answer depends on the Reynolds number you compute in Section 5.6, not on which instrument looked most expensive.

3Safety

  • Fluids heated to 50 – 60 °C will burn skin. Use gloves and tongs on the Saybolt cup and the rotary beaker.
  • Wipe every spill at once. Oil on a hard floor is a fall hazard.
  • Do not heat any fluid above the temperature stated in the procedure.
  • Recover the steel sphere with the magnet. Never invert the falling ball tube.
  • Used fluid goes in the marked waste container, never down the sink.
  • Report a cracked tube or a leaking cup to the instructor before proceeding.

5Part I — Falling Ball Viscometer

One of the oldest ways of measuring the viscosity of a fluid is to measure how fast a sphere falls through it. A sphere released in a viscous fluid accelerates for a short distance and then settles at a constant terminal velocity, where the drag force exactly balances the sphere's weight less its buoyancy. If the sphere falls quickly the fluid has low viscosity and little resistance to flow; if it falls slowly the fluid has high viscosity. Stokes' law relates that settling velocity to viscosity, rearranged below to solve for viscosity.

μ = 2 Δρ g r² 9 v
(1)
μ   absolute (dynamic) viscosity, Pa·s
Δρ   density of sphere minus density of fluid, kg/m³
g   acceleration due to gravity, 9.81 m/s²
r   radius of sphere, m
v   velocity of sphere, m/s
Release the sphere above the START mark
Equation (1) applies only to a sphere already at terminal velocity. If you release the sphere at the START mark it is still accelerating as it crosses it, your timed velocity is too low, and your viscosity comes out too high. Release it at the top of the tube and let it settle before it reaches the mark.

6Part II — Saybolt Viscometer

The Saybolt viscometer measures fluid flow through a capillary of very small diameter. Before filling the vessel, secure the rubber stopper at the base. The container must be filled just to overflowing for accurate and consistent results, because the height of fluid above the orifice sets the driving pressure. Position the beaker underneath and begin timing at the instant the stopper is removed. Record the time in Saybolt Universal Seconds required for 60 mL to collect in the beaker.

Heat 60 mL of each fluid to 50 °C, pour it into the viscometer, and record the collection time.

7Part III — Rotary Viscometer

A rotary viscometer measures the absolute viscosity of a fluid directly. A rotor immersed in the sample is driven at a set speed, and the torque required to maintain that speed is proportional to the fluid's viscosity. Sensors inside the instrument measure that torque and the viscosity is displayed on the screen. The measurement is easier to perform and generally more accurate than the other two methods, which is what makes it a useful reference against your falling-ball result.

Identify the components of the instrument and become familiar with the values shown on the display. Fill a beaker with 500 mL of fluid and heat it to 60 °C. Record the temperature and the displayed viscosity in mPa·s at 5 °C intervals as the sample cools, and convert each reading to Pa·s using Equation (9).

μ [Pa·s] = μ [mPa·s] 1000 (1 Pa·s = 1000 mPa·s)
(9)

7.1What the instrument computes

The display gives you a viscosity directly, but it is worth knowing what stands behind that number, because it explains why the spindle and the speed must be recorded. Treat the rotor as a cylinder of radius Ri turning at angular velocity ω inside a stationary beaker of inner radius Ro, immersed to a depth H. The instrument holds the speed constant and measures the torque M required to maintain it.

ω = 2πN 60 γ̇ = 2ωRo² Ro² − Ri²
(10)
τ = M 2πRi²H μ = τ γ̇
(11)

Combining the two gives the viscosity in terms of the measured torque alone:

μ = M (Ro² − Ri²) 4πωHRi²Ro²
(12)
N   rotation speed, rev/min    ω   angular velocity, rad/s
Ri   rotor radius, m    Ro   beaker inner radius, m
H   immersed height of the rotor, m    M   torque on the rotor, N·m
γ̇   shear rate, s−1    τ   shear stress, Pa

Every quantity in Equation (12) except M and N is fixed once the spindle and beaker are chosen, so the whole expression collapses to μ = KM/N, where K is a pure geometric constant. That constant is the spindle factor printed in the instrument's tables, and it is the reason a reading is meaningless unless the spindle and the speed are recorded alongside it.

Assumptions behind Equation (12)
The fluid is Newtonian, the flow is laminar, the rotor is fully immersed and turning coaxially in the beaker, and the torque contributed by the top and bottom faces of the rotor is neglected. Equation (12) is exact for a long cylinder in a close-fitting beaker and becomes an approximation as the gap widens.
Compute for one of your readings:   ω = rad/s    γ̇ = s−1    M = N·m

Record the rotor and the speed before you take any reading. The torque the instrument senses depends on the rotor diameter, its immersed height, the clearance to the beaker wall and the rotation speed, so a viscosity value taken on this bench is only reproducible by someone else if all of them are written down. A reading quoted without its spindle and speed is not a measurement, it is a number.

8Analysis Questions

  1. Which of your two fluids has the greater resistance to flow? Cite the specific measured quantity that establishes this, not the calculated viscosity alone.
  2. Your falling-ball result is a dynamic viscosity and your Saybolt result begins as a kinematic viscosity. Explain why the Saybolt method yields kinematic viscosity directly, given that the fluid is driven through the orifice by its own weight.
  3. Compare the viscosity you obtained for the same fluid by the falling-ball method and by the rotary viscometer at the same temperature. Express the disagreement as a percentage of the rotary value.
  4. List two experimental causes of the disagreement in Question 3 that are independent of the validity of Stokes' law.
  5. Using your Reynolds numbers from Section 5.6, state whether Stokes' law was applicable to each fluid. For any fluid where it was not, state the direction of the resulting error: at Reynolds numbers above one, drag on the sphere is larger than Equation (1) assumes, so does your computed viscosity read high or low? Justify from Equation (1).
  6. Propose one change to the falling-ball apparatus that would bring the Reynolds number into the valid range, and estimate the factor by which it would reduce Re. Note that v is proportional to r², so Re is proportional to r³.
  7. Describe the shape of your temperature-viscosity curve. Is a straight line an adequate description of it across the whole range? What does your answer imply about quoting a single viscosity value for a hydraulic fluid without stating a temperature?
  8. A hydraulic system designed around a fluid at 40 °C is started on a winter morning at 5 °C. Using the trend in your data, argue whether the pump inlet or the actuator is the more likely place for trouble, and say what physically goes wrong there.
  9. An engine oil is labelled 15W-40 and a hydraulic fluid is labelled ISO 46. State which viscosity family each grading system refers to and at what temperature, and explain why the two labels cannot be compared directly.
  10. Of your three measurements of the same fluid, which do you trust most? Defend the choice using your uncertainty estimate from Section 5.5 and your Reynolds numbers from Section 5.6, not the reputation of the instrument.
