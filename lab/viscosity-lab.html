Viscosity Measurement Laboratory

Fluid Power Systems & Internal Combustion Engines

Laboratory Objectives

Three instruments, one property. Each station measures the same physical quantity by a different route, and the three routes do not always agree. Explaining the disagreement is the point of the laboratory.

Objectives

  • Become familiar with viscosity measurement methods.
  • Measure viscosity of hydraulic fluids with falling ball viscometer, Saybolt viscometer and rotary viscometer.
  • Convert one set of viscosity units to another set of units.
  • Analyze the effect of temperature on viscosity of hydraulic fluid.

Core Principle

Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to flow. Every station on this bench measures that resistance indirectly: by how fast a sphere sinks, by how long a fixed volume takes to drain through an orifice, or by how hard a rotor must be driven.

Dynamic viscosity μ and kinematic viscosity ν are not the same quantity. Density connects them. A conversion between the two families is impossible without a density, and this application will refuse to perform one.

Safety and Laboratory Notes

  • Hydraulic fluid and oil heated to 60 °C will burn skin. Use gloves and tongs.
  • Wipe spills immediately. Oil on a floor is a fall hazard.
  • Do not heat fluids above the temperature stated in the procedure.
  • Retrieve the steel sphere with the magnet, never by inverting the tube.
  • Dispose of used fluid in the marked waste container, not the sink.

Station 1 — Falling Ball Viscometer

A sphere released in a viscous fluid accelerates only briefly, then falls at a steady terminal velocity where drag balances net weight. Timing that steady fall over a known distance gives the velocity, and Stokes' law converts velocity into viscosity.

    Station 2 — Saybolt Viscometer

    The Saybolt method times how long a fixed volume takes to drain through a standardized orifice. The reading is a time in Saybolt Universal Seconds, not a viscosity, and it must be converted. Because the method measures flow under the fluid's own weight, it yields kinematic viscosity directly; density is needed to reach dynamic viscosity.

    Formula Reference — Saybolt
    32 < SUS < 100
    cSt = 0.226 SUS − 195 / SUS
    SUS > 100
    cSt = 0.220 SUS − 135 / SUS
    Dynamic from kinematic
    cP = cSt × ρ [g/cm³]
    Note
    Both expressions give 20.65 cSt at SUS = 100, so the pair is continuous at the boundary.
    t = 0.0 s

    Why this works

    A narrower or more viscous stream takes longer to deliver the same 60 mL. The orifice geometry is fixed by the standard, so the drain time carries all the information about the fluid. Because gravity supplies the driving force and gravity scales with density, the raw result is kinematic viscosity, which is exactly viscosity divided by density.
    WHAT THE ANIMATION SHOWS The flask fills at a uniform rate over the efflux time. A real cup drains a little more slowly as it empties, because the head above the orifice falls. The quantity being reproduced here is the total time for 60 mL, which is what a Saybolt reading is, not the shape of the flow curve.
    PROCEDURE REMINDER Fill to overflowing before starting, and start the stopwatch at the instant the stopper is pulled. An underfilled cup lowers the head and lengthens the time.

    Station 3 — Rotary Viscometer

    A rotor turning in the fluid at a set speed meets a resisting torque proportional to viscosity. The instrument measures that torque and displays dynamic viscosity directly in mPa·s. This is the station where temperature dependence is easiest to see, because the fluid can be held at a series of set temperatures while readings are taken.

    Formula Reference — Rotary Viscometer
    Angular velocity from the set speed
    ω = 2πN60   rad/s, N in rev/min
    Shear rate at the rotor surface
    γ̇ = 2ω Ro² Ro² − Ri²
    Shear stress at the rotor surface
    τ = M 2π Ri² H
    Viscosity, the definition the instrument applies
    μ = τ γ̇
    Combined, solved for viscosity (Margules)
    μ = M (Ro² − Ri²) 4π ω H Ri² Ro²
    The same relation solved for torque
    M = 4π μ ω H Ri² Ro² Ro² − Ri²
    Wide beaker, Ro » Ri
    γ̇ → 2ω    M → 4πμωH Ri²    μ → M 4πωH Ri²
    Why an instrument quotes a spindle constant
    Everything except M and N is fixed once the spindle and beaker are chosen, so the relation collapses to μ = K M / N, with K a pure geometric constant. That K is the spindle factor printed in an instrument's tables.
    Units
    1 Pa·s = 1000 mPa·s = 1000 cP  ·  ν = μ / ρ
    Symbols
    Ri rotor radius, Ro beaker inner radius, H immersed height of the rotor, M torque on the rotor, all in SI. Valid for a Newtonian fluid in laminar flow with the rotor fully immersed and coaxial, neglecting torque on the rotor ends.

    DYNAMIC ≠ KINEMATIC ν = μ / ρ. Two fluids with equal dynamic viscosity but different densities have different kinematic viscosities, and vice versa. An ISO grade quotes kinematic viscosity in cSt at 40 °C; a Stokes' law result is dynamic in Pa·s. They are not interchangeable.
